\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

The results of \cref{sec:results} are interpreted here with focus on the five research
questions from \cref{sec:research-questions}. One caveat applies to everything: the test
lab is small, with four SR Linux nodes, four clients and only one injected fault at a
time. Additionally, each problem is described by only one open prompt. There is no real human interacting (giving directions or generally more information)
with the agent system. The results show what the system can do in such an environment. This work does not claim the same results would be achieved for
large enterprise networks (see \cref{sec:eval-limitations}).

\subsection{Success Rate by Difficulty}
\label{sec:eval-success}

Three patterns stand out in the correctness numbers
(\cref{tab:found-rate-tier}, \cref{fig:found-rate-scenario}).

\paragraph{High tier} With 98 of 100 runs correct, the
difficulty tiers for the problems barely matter for GPT-5.5 and Claude Opus 4.8. Hard scenarios such as the MTU
blackhole or the one-way route filter were found as reliably as a downed interface. Within this
lab, the answer to the difficulty question is that all difficulty tiers from the defined scenarios can be
troubleshot, but only the most expensive models handle every tier reliably.

\paragraph{Mid tier} GLM-5.2 and
Qwen3.7-Max are reliable on easy faults, handling that tier almost perfectly (28 of 30 runs),
but they miss runs on medium and hard scenarios. Their misses concentrate on \texttt{duplicate-ip-arp} and
\texttt{acl-silent-drop}, and most came from running out of time rather than from a
wrong conclusion. 16 of their 22 misses are runs that hit the 600-second cap without
delivering a final answer and only two ended in an actual wrong diagnosis. (Four of GLM-5.2's
misses have a completely different cause, runs that ended without any investigation, see
\cref{sec:eval-limitations}.)
The nominally hard
\texttt{mtu-blackhole} scenario was not a problem for them, so the manually
assigned difficulty tiers only partially predict what the models actually struggle with.
No single characteristic was identified that reliably predicts whether a scenario is truly difficult for an LLM.


\paragraph{Low tier} DeepSeek-V3.2 and Ministral-14B are not usable for this task (network troubleshooting) based on the results, and they
fail in different ways. DeepSeek works slowly and often does not finish: 38 of its 50 runs hit the
600-second cap. Ministral finishes quickly but is wrong, which is even worse. It answers within about a minute, yet
mostly names the wrong cause or none. Its mean of 24 tool calls per run
(\cref{fig:tool-calls}) shows why. It stops gathering information very quickly and bases the final response on little information.
The healthy baseline scenario shows the same split:
DeepSeek timed out without an answer in 4 of its 5 baseline runs, while Ministral reported a
fault that did not exist in 3 of its 5 (\cref{sec:results-correctness}). In real operation, such
false alarms would cost engineer time instead of saving it.

The combined per-tier success rates (70--78\,\% baseline/easy, 54--61\,\% medium/hard)
average over six models of very different ability, so they mainly reflect the composition of
the model set rather than the difficulty tiers themselves. The
split by model in \cref{tab:found-rate-tier} is the more informative view, and it shows that the
success rate depends far more on the model than on the scenario difficulty.

\subsection{Time to Diagnosis}
\label{sec:eval-time}

For the time question the relevant metric is the wall-clock time from query to a correct
final answer (\cref{fig:time-to-diagnosis}). Wrong diagnoses are excluded since they save no time,
and someone has to notice and correct them. By this metric a correct
diagnosis takes about two minutes with Claude Opus 4.8, three with GPT-5.5 and four to
four-and-a-half with the mid-tier models. Ministral's one-minute median looks impressive but
covers only the 8 runs it got right, and DeepSeek's correct runs took over seven minutes. The
time also barely increases with difficulty. For the high-tier models a hard scenario takes only
about 40--50 seconds longer than an easy one (\cref{sec:results-time}).

Part of why the wall-clock time stays this low is the parallelism of the architecture. For
every model, the LLM inference time summed over all round-trips exceeds the wall-clock time of
the run (\cref{fig:inference-time}). The orchestrator can launch multiple sub-agents, which can issue their requests
concurrently, so more model time is consumed than passes on the clock. Qwen3.7-Max shows the
largest gap (a mean of roughly 480\,s of summed inference against 325\,s of wall clock), meaning it
made the most use of parallel sub-agents.

No human control group was used in this project, so the comparison with required manual troubleshooting time
is based on an estimate. For the injected faults, a plausible range for a network
operator (reproduce the symptom, work through the devices, find the cause) is in the range
of 10--15 minutes for the easy tier, 30--60 minutes for the medium tier and about one to two hours for
the hard tier. Against that estimate the agent
system saves little time on trivial faults (a downed interface is quick to find either way) but,
even after adding a few minutes of operator time to read and verify the report,
potentially 90\,\% or more time on medium and hard faults, where its time stays
flat at a few minutes while manual effort grows. This is only true when the diagnosis is correct,
which for the observed models means the high price tier. At a 74--82\,\% success rate a network operator would still have to check every answer before acting on it.

A related observation concerns the healthy baseline. For some models, confirming that nothing
is wrong took as long as or longer than locating an easy fault (Qwen3.7-Max: 477\,s versus
172\,s, \cref{sec:results-time}). That makes sense. A fault search
can stop at the first plausible cause it finds, whereas declaring a network healthy requires ruling out every
possibility. In practice this is very relevant since some problems might not be related to the network and the result should indicate this. 

\subsection{Cost}
\label{sec:eval-cost}

Cost is best compared per correct diagnosis, a model's total spend divided by its number of
correct diagnoses (the ``cost/correct'' column of \cref{tab:model-summary}), since a wrong
diagnosis has no value.
Per correct diagnosis, GLM-5.2 is the cheapest usable option at \$0.21, Claude Opus 4.8 costs
\$1.22 and GPT-5.5 \$2.91. DeepSeek-V3.2 illustrates why cost per run is misleading on its own.
It is as cheap as GLM-5.2, but because it rarely succeeds, each correct answer
effectively costs three times as much. In the cost--success plane of \cref{fig:cost-vs-correctness}, the found rate rises monotonically
with cost, so every model buys some amount of additional found rate for its extra cost and none
is simply worse on both axes than a cheaper one. The two low-tier models are very cheap, but their
found rate is too low to be usable.

\paragraph{A saving estimate} As an example we assume the cost of CHF 100 per network operator hour. 
With the manual-time estimates from \cref{sec:eval-time}, diagnosing a medium fault
manually costs around CHF 50--100 and a hard one CHF 100--200. The same diagnosis by the agent
system costs \$1--3 in API fees with a high-tier model plus a few minutes of network operator time to
read and verify the report. This is estimated at CHF 10--15 in total. Even under these assumptions
the potential saving per medium or hard incident is roughly a factor of three to twenty, depending on
the tier and the assumed manual time. The
saving comes mostly from the replaced network operator time. The choice of model matters little here (in terms of cost),
since the API fee is small compared to a single network operator hour either way. This is an
illustration rather than an evidence-based measurement, because the operator hours are only estimates.

\paragraph{Price volatility} Two constraints apply to the cost figures. First, they are
modelled from token counts and the models' advertised OpenRouter prices at run time and not from
OpenRouter's billing system. Provider routing and prompt caching can make the billed amount differ, typically
downward because cached prompt tokens are not billed by a provider that applies caching.
Caching was therefore excluded deliberately from the cost calculation because its availability varies by provider and model and is often
best-effort, which would have made the cross-model comparison depend on routing luck and on whether a model even does implicit caching.
Second, OpenRouter prices are volatile (based on provider) especially for open models, where multiple providers compete for the
same model. During the window in which the experiments were run, GLM-5.2's input price
moved from \$0.95 to \$0.94 and then \$0.93 per million tokens (\cref{app:model-prices}). Three days after GLM-5.2's last run, on 6 July
2026, its advertised price was about 27\,\% lower than what the runs were logged at. The proprietary models
(Opus 4.8, GPT-5.5, Qwen3.7-Max) kept a constant list price throughout. The exact rates in effect
were recovered from the logs and are listed in \cref{app:model-prices}. Repricing the entire
experiment at the rates of 6 July 2026 changes the total by under 1\,\%, so the comparisons above
are robust to this change. 

\subsection{Influence of the Model}
\label{sec:eval-model}

Across all metrics, model choice is a bigger factor than scenario difficulty. The found rate
spans 16\,\% to 100\,\% across the model price tiers, while within one model the difference between
easy and hard scenarios is comparatively small. The price tiers from \cref{sec:model-tier-concept}
translate into three distinct behaviours. The high tier (Opus 4.8, GPT-5.5) reaches
near-perfect success on every difficulty tier, at \$1.22--\$2.91 per correct diagnosis versus
\$0.21--\$0.55 for the mid tier, roughly 2--14 times as much depending on the pairing. It also
invents no faults on the healthy baseline. The
mid tier (GLM-5.2, Qwen3.7-Max) is the cost-effectiveness sweet spot for easy faults, but
its 8 DNFs per model are concentrated on \texttt{duplicate-ip-arp} (medium tier) and
\texttt{acl-silent-drop} (hard tier), with a few more spread across the baseline and easy
scenarios. In almost all of these misses the model ran out of time rather than answering wrongly.
The low tier
(DeepSeek-V3.2, Ministral-14B) is not usable: one model is too slow to finish, the other is fast
but usually wrong, and both are unreliable on the healthy baseline scenario.

The models also took different paths to their results. GPT-5.5 reaches its
perfect found rate with heavy tool use, a mean of 124 tool calls and 375\,000 normalised input tokens
per run (\cref{fig:tool-calls}, \cref{fig:token-usage}). Claude Opus 4.8 reaches a nearly
identical rate with about a third of the tool calls and less than a quarter of the input
tokens. This gap in tokens used is what mainly
drives the difference in cost per correct diagnosis (\$2.91 versus \$1.22) -- the two models are
priced similarly per token, so GPT-5.5 would remain more expensive even at Opus 4.8's token price.

Robustness follows the same ordering with one exception, the number of malformed device commands
per run (\cref{sec:results-cost}) is lowest for Ministral and the high tier and by far the
highest for Qwen3.7-Max. Ministral's low count is partly a consequence of its short
runs: a model that issues few commands has few opportunities to use an invalid one. The ability
to issue syntactically valid commands and the ability to
diagnose correctly appear to be independent.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:eval-limitations}

The results must be considered within the limits of the experiment.

The lab is small and single-vendor: four Nokia SR Linux nodes and four clients, with exactly
one injected fault at a time. An enterprise network has many more devices,
mixed vendors, concurrent faults and noisy alarms. Nothing here shows how the system scales
to that. The results are a feasibility statement for small environments and not for big enterprise networks.
The runs are also non-interactive, each consists of a single symptom
description with no follow-up dialogue (\cref{sec:experiment-execution}). In a real use case,
a network operator could answer questions the agents raise, add observations, or steer the
investigation. Such help would plausibly raise the success rate, particularly for the models
that stopped at a plausible but wrong explanation. The reported numbers measure the fully
autonomous mode.

Three restrictions have to be considered regarding the measurements. The time and cost savings compare against an estimate
of how long an engineer would need. No human trials were run. Each scenario--model pair was
repeated only five times, so one additional or one fewer successful run shifts a rate by 20 percentage points.
And the decision whether a run was a success or a miss was made by one person against a fixed criterion. Borderline answers
could be judged differently by another person.

Two further restrictions concern the recorded data. First, GLM-5.2's success rate may be
understated. In 4 of its 50 runs the model ended the agent loop after a single response
without calling any tools (the reasoning stated that tools should be called, but none were), so no investigation took place (\cref{sec:results-correctness}),
all four count as misses. Excluding these degenerate runs, its found rate would be 37/46
($\approx$80\,\%) instead of 74\,\%. GLM-5.2 had been released not long before the
experiment window, so issues in its tool-calling layer are a plausible cause. This means the reported 74\,\% may understate the model's actual diagnostic ability.
Secondly, runs were capped at 600 seconds, and this cap does not weigh equally on all models.
The cap also shapes the difficulty answer for the mid tier: 16 of the mid-tier models' 22
misses are cap hits rather than wrong conclusions, so with a longer time budget their picture
on the medium and hard scenarios could look better.
Token generation speed differs between models, and for the open-weight models it
is partly a property of the serving provider (compute power) rather than of the model itself. A slowly served
model reaches the time limit, without being able to do as much work as a fast one, independent
of its diagnostic ability. DeepSeek-V3.2 is the clearest case. A large part of its timeouts can be attributed to slow
token generation rather than to a deeper investigation, since its tool-call count is close to
GLM-5.2's (\cref{fig:tool-calls}) while far more of its runs hit the cap, so its low
found rate partly reflects serving speed instead of only diagnostic capability. Its true time
and success numbers beyond the cap are unknown (more than half of its runs were cut off). Token and cost figures for DNF
runs are even a bit undercounted. Usage is recorded only when an agent run returns, so the cap
discards the orchestrator's own consumption and that of any sub-agent still working,
counting only the sub-agents that had already completed. The reported cost of models with many
DNFs understates what they actually consumed.
